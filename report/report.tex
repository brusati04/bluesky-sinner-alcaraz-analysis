\begin{abstract}
This report explores how the tennis rivalry between \textbf{Jannik Sinner} and \textbf{Carlos Alcaraz} shaped the online discourse on the \textbf{Bluesky} social platform during the \textbf{2025 US Open}. Gathering a dataset of \textbf{9{,}078 posts} via a day-by-day crawling strategy, we reconstruct the network of replies and mentions to map how communities formed, while tracing the emotional and sentiment dynamics triggered by match outcomes. The analysis reveals a highly modular network where fans cluster around shared news hubs rather than forming isolated echo chambers. Furthermore, by integrating neural sentiment signals with user interaction frequencies, we demonstrate how a sentiment-aware stance classifier can successfully refine and correct the classification errors inherent in simpler, frequency-only baselines.

\end{abstract}

\section{Research Question}

The goal of this project is to \textbf{characterise the online discourse} surrounding two of
tennis's biggest rivals---Jannik Sinner and Carlos Alcaraz---on the Bluesky social platform
during the 2025 US Open. In doing so, this study is guided by the following overarching \textbf{research question}:

\begin{center}
    \vspace{0.8cm}
    \large
    \textit{``How do the Jannik Sinner and Carlos Alcaraz fan communities on Bluesky manifest at both a structural-topological and emotional level during a Grand Slam tournament like the US Open?''}
    \vspace{0.8cm}
\end{center}

To address this research question, we build a modular, reproducible NLP and social-network analysis pipeline. Concretely, the analysis aims to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Describe} the social-network structure built from replies and mentions,
    identify cohesive communities, and measure user influence via centrality metrics.
    \item \textbf{Quantify} sentiment and emotion expressed in posts using state-of-the-art
    NLP models (RoBERTa, NRC lexicon, GoEmotions) and compare two emotion backends.
    \item \textbf{Assign a stance} (pro-Sinner, pro-Alcaraz or neutral) to each user by fusing
    sentiment signals with mention frequency, and evaluate how this refined stance differs from a
    simpler frequency-only baseline.
    \item \textbf{Visualise} all results to support interpretability---network graphs, sentiment
    time series, emotion profiles and stance distributions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Data}

\subsection{Collection strategy}

Data were collected from Bluesky using its official \texttt{atproto} API
(\texttt{src/crawler.py}). Because the search endpoint does not guarantee exhaustive retrieval
over a large window, a \textbf{day-by-day} crawling strategy was used:

\begin{itemize}
    \item For each 24-hour window from \texttt{2025-08-21} to \texttt{2025-09-10} (UTC), two
    keyword queries were issued---one \textbf{Sinner-oriented} and one
    \textbf{Alcaraz-oriented}---each capped at \textbf{5{,}000 posts/day} to respect rate limits
    while capturing peak tournament activity.
    \item Results from both queries were merged and deduplicated by post URI.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Preprocessing}

The raw CSV goes through a preprocessing phase to be transformed into an enriched dataset
(\texttt{data/sinner\_alcaraz\_processed.csv}, 44 columns) that serves as the single source of
truth for all downstream stages. The enrichment (\texttt{src/preprocessing.py}) includes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Text cleaning} (HTML unescape, URL/handle removal, normalisation) and
    \textbf{lemmatisation} (spaCy + TweetTokenizer).
    \item \textbf{RoBERTa sentiment} via
    \texttt{cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-sentiment-latest}, producing a
    \texttt{sentiment\_category} (positive/neutral/negative) and a continuous
    \texttt{sentiment\_compound} $= P(\text{pos}) - P(\text{neg})$.
    \item \textbf{Emotion} with two backends: the \textbf{NRC lexicon} (NRCLex) over the 8
    Plutchik emotions, and \textbf{GoEmotions} (\texttt{SamLowe/roberta-base-go\_emotions}),
    whose 28 fine-grained labels are collapsed onto the same 8 categories for comparison.
    \item \textbf{Named entity recognition} (spaCy) and \textbf{entity linking} to detect
    mentions of Sinner or Alcaraz.
    \item \textbf{Post- and user-level stance} (see Section~\ref{sec:stancemethod}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Methodology}

The analytical pipeline is a strict DAG (orchestrated by \texttt{main.py}).

\subsection{Network construction}

Two graphs are built:
\begin{itemize}
    \item an undirected graph $G_u$, where the edge weight is the number of interactions between users;
    \item a directed graph $G_d$ that captures the flow of communication from an author to the exact user they are replying to or mentioning.
\end{itemize}

For both, we compute degree, closeness and betweenness centrality, in-/out-degree, and PageRank.

To understand how these users naturally cluster together, we apply the Louvain community detection algorithm to the undirected graph, while using the Infomap algorithm to detect communities within the directed graph.

Filtered subgraphs (we selected the top 5 largest communities) are used for visualisation and stance analysis.

\subsection{Stance detection}
\label{sec:stancemethod}

Post-level stance assigns the RoBERTa compound score to the mentioned player for single-mention
posts; dual-mention posts are split via sentence-level sentiment. Similarly, post-level emotion stance assigns the net GoEmotions (BERT) emotion valence (the sum of positive emotions: joy, trust, and anticipation, minus the sum of negative emotions: anger, disgust, sadness, and fear) to the mentioned player.
User-level net stance fuses mean sentiment, mention frequency, and mean emotion stance:

\[
\text{net\_stance} = 0.50\,(\text{mean}_\text{s} - \text{mean}_\text{a})
                   + 0.15\,(\text{freq}_\text{s} - \text{freq}_\text{a})
                   + 0.35\,(\text{mean\_emo}_\text{s} - \text{mean\_emo}_\text{a})
\]

Users are labelled \texttt{sinner} / \texttt{alcaraz} / \texttt{neutral} with a $\pm 0.05$ threshold.
Polarisation is measured via stance assortativity, the cross-stance edge ratio, and the standard
deviation of net stance.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Social Network Analysis}
\label{sec:netresults}

The interaction graph is \textbf{sparse but strongly modular}. 

Out of 2{,}296 active users in the dataset, 683 engaged in interactions (replies or mentions) 
and form the nodes of our interaction network, while the remaining 1{,}613 users posted standalone 
content without direct interactive links. Global metrics (Table~\ref{tab:global}) 
computed on this interactive subset report a density of $\approx 0.002$ for the undirected graph 
and $\approx 0.001$ for the directed graph. Modularity scores are extremely high (Louvain $\approx 0.97$, 
Infomap $\approx 0.94$), suggesting communities cluster tightly around neutral-news-hub users rather than mixing. 
The giant connected component (GCC/WCC) spans only \textbf{111 users ($\approx$16.3\% of the interaction network)}, 
indicating that much of the interactive conversation happens in small, loosely linked clusters.

\subsubsection{Emotion Analysis: NRCLex vs GoEmotions}
\label{sec:emotionresults}

Comparing the two emotion backends exposes a decisive advantage of the transformer model
(Figure~\ref{fig:emocompare}). NRCLex is a \textbf{bag-of-words} model: it looks up each word
in an internal dictionary and checks which emotion the word is associated with. This causes
posts mentioning \textbf{Sinner} to be classified under negative emotions (\texttt{fear},
\texttt{anger}, \dots) because the lexicon interprets the word ``sinner'' as the religious sense
of \emph{sin}. \textbf{GoEmotions (BERT)}, by contrast, is a transformer that resolves
\emph{Sinner the tennis player} from \emph{sinner the sin} from context. The effect is clearly
visible in the dominant-emotion distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:emobackend}): BERT assigns
essentially \textbf{nobody} to \texttt{fear}, collapsing the spurious negative mass that NRC
produces and reallocating it to \texttt{neutral} and \texttt{trust}.

\subsection{Social Stance Analysis}
\label{sec:stanceresults}

Fusing sentiment, mention frequency and emotion into the user-level net stance
(Section~\ref{sec:stancemethod}) yields the picture summarised in
Figure~\ref{fig:stancesummary}. Of the \textbf{2{,}296} users analysed, \textbf{45.1\%}
(1{,}035) are neutral, \textbf{36.4\%} (835) lean towards Alcaraz and \textbf{18.6\%} (426)
towards Sinner. \emph{Alcaraz dominates the space}: his fanbase is nearly double the size of
Sinner's.

From the \emph{Top Communities -- Mean Stance Score} panel we can see that the largest
communities are predominantly neutral; only two opposing communities take a clear position,
delineating one pro-Sinner community and one opposing pro-Alcaraz community.

Crucially, \textbf{no echo chambers exist}. Stance assortativity is slightly \emph{negative}
($-0.071$) and \textbf{14.7\%} of edges cross partisan lines (cross-stance edge ratio $0.147$,
net-stance standard deviation $0.327$). Fans of opposing players actively interact with one
another, bridged by shared, neutral sports-news hubs rather than retreating into isolated
clusters.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{stance_analysis_summary.png}
\caption{Stance analysis summary: user stance distribution, net-stance score distribution,
per-community mean stance, and polarisation metrics.}

\paragraph{Why mention frequency alone is not enough.}
Relying on mention frequency alone misclassifies users, because mentioning a player frequently
says nothing about whether the user \emph{supports} or \emph{criticises} that player. Fusing
sentiment with frequency is therefore methodologically essential to capture true support versus
criticism.

Projecting the fused stance onto the directed graph (Figure~\ref{fig:netfanbase}) makes the
spatial organisation of support explicit. Nodes coloured \textbf{orange} indicate a stance
favourable to (or focused on) Sinner, while \textbf{blue} nodes represent an orientation towards
Alcaraz. The two leanings are interleaved throughout the giant component rather than separated
into distinct partisan regions, visually confirming the absence of echo chambers reported above.

\subsubsection{Sentiment dynamics over time}
\label{sec:timeresults}

Tracking daily average sentiment against post volume across the tournament
(Figure~\ref{fig:senttime}) reveals four clear patterns:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High event reactivity.} Online sentiment is purely reactive: match outcomes
    instantly shift the sentiment curves of both fanbases day by day.
    \item \textbf{The final divergence.} The final on \textbf{7 September} splits the emotional
    tone of the two communities: Alcaraz supporters reach their positive-sentiment peak (his
    victory), while Sinner supporters plunge towards a neutral-low score.
    \item \textbf{Civility under defeat.} Despite the disappointment of losing the final, the
    Sinner fanbase sentiment never drops deep into negative territory, indicating that
    disappointment did not turn into toxic behaviour or online hostility.
    \item \textbf{Late-tournament hype.} Engagement is highly concentrated: post volume grows
    exponentially during the final rounds (quarterfinals onwards), while the early rounds
    generate relatively low traction.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Taken together, the results answer the research question on both the structural-topological and
the emotional level.

\paragraph{Structural-topological level.}
The Sinner and Alcaraz communities do \emph{not} manifest as antagonistic echo chambers. The
network is highly modular ($Q \approx 0.94$--$0.97$), yet that modularity reflects topical and
hub-centred clustering rather than partisan segregation: stance assortativity is slightly
negative ($-0.071$) and roughly one edge in seven crosses partisan lines
(Section~\ref{sec:stanceresults}). The comparison between Louvain and Infomap
(Section~\ref{sec:netresults}) is itself a finding: the choice of a \emph{directed} model is
what exposes the real information flow: where the undirected view sees a single dense
macro-community, the directed view resolves an articulated internal structure of functional
sub-groups bridged by neutral news hubs. These hubs, not partisan accounts, are the structural
backbone that keeps the opposing fanbases connected.

\paragraph{Emotional level.}
Emotionally, the discourse is dominated by neutral and trust-laden content rather than hostility.
Sentiment is strongly event-driven: it tracks match outcomes day by day and diverges sharply at
the final, yet even in defeat the Sinner fanbase remains civil and never tips into sustained
negativity (Section~\ref{sec:timeresults}). The stance analysis adds nuance to this affective
picture: Alcaraz commands the larger and more positively-charged fanbase, while a substantial
neutral majority (45.1\%) consumes and relays tournament news without taking sides.

\paragraph{Methodological contribution.}
Two methodological points stand out. First, \textbf{model choice matters for emotion}: the
``sinner''$\to$``sin'' lexical trap shows that a bag-of-words backend (NRC) systematically
fabricates \texttt{fear}/\texttt{anger} signal, whereas a context-aware transformer
(GoEmotions/BERT) disambiguates the player's name and yields a far more faithful emotion
distribution. Second, \textbf{stance requires more than counting mentions}: mention frequency
alone conflates attention with allegiance, and only by fusing sentiment and emotion with
frequency does the classifier recover genuine support versus criticism
(Section~\ref{sec:stanceresults}).

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.}
There are a few limitations to this study. One core issue is the time window: the crawl covers only 
the US Open, which is a shared, high-salience event that may suppress echo-chamber dynamics.
Future work should extend the window to test whether the absence of echo chambers persists outside 
of this context. Another limitation is the reliance on English-only sentiment and emotion models,
which likely inflates the neutral share and understates genuine affect in non-English posts; but also 
greatly reduces the amount of data that can be meaningfully analysed, also possibly not considering 
parts of communities that predominantly post in other languages. 
Future improvements should add language detection and multilingual models to sharpen both sentiment
curves and stance scores. There is also the still-open research question on how to handle at best
sarcasm and irony, which are common in sports discourse and can easily mislead sentiment classifiers. 
Finally, the stance fusion formula is currently heuristic; future work could explore more complex 
approaches.

\subsection{Conclusion}

To answer the research question, this study characterised the Sinner--Alcaraz discourse on Bluesky 
during the 2025 US~Open by combining directed/undirected community detection, neural sentiment
and emotion analysis, and a sentiment-aware stance classifier over a dataset of 9{,}078 posts. 
The picture that emerges is of a \textbf{highly modular but non-polarised} community: fans cluster 
around shared news hubs rather than partisan echo chambers, opposing fanbases interact across stance
lines, and the emotional tone---while sharply reactive to match outcomes---stays predominantly neutral
and civil, even in defeat. Methodologically, the work demonstrates that a context-aware transformer
is necessary to avoid lexical artefacts in emotion detection, and that fusing sentiment with
interaction frequency meaningfully outperforms a frequency-only stance baseline, recovering genuine
support from mere mention.
